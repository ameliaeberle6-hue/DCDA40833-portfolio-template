Exploring patterns with Plotly

Interactive Charts (Plotly)

Select countries to compare their FIFA ranking points over time. This chart is generated locally using the cleaned CSV included in the repository.

Reflection & Analysis

1. Data Preparation

I selected a dataset I am familiar with: the FIFA world ranking dataset (international men's football rankings). Preparing data for visualization can fail for common reasons: malformed CSV (unescaped quotes, inconsistent column counts), missing header row, or unexpected character encodings. To prepare the file I inspected the CSV in a text editor and loaded it into Python to verify consistent columns and UTF-8 encoding. I removed extraneous rows (notes or metadata at the top) and ensured dates were in ISO format (YYYY-MM-DD) for proper parsing. For interactive charts, I verified data types and handled missing values appropriately.

2. Design Choices

For the visualization I focused on patterns in national team ranking changes over time. The primary metric is the FIFA ranking points and the derived metric of rank change (delta from previous ranking). Dimensions include Country (discrete), Date (continuous), and Region/Confederation as a grouping variable. I chose a line chart with multiple small multiples — one line per country — arranged by confederation, allowing comparison of temporal trends. For highlight interactions I configured tooltips to show rank, points, and recent match results (when available). Color encodes confederation to surface regional patterns, while line thickness highlights the top-tier teams to focus attention.

3. Expected Patterns

Based on my domain knowledge, I expected established football powers (e.g., Brazil, Germany, Argentina) to show relatively stable, high ranking points with occasional dips after poor tournament performance. Emerging teams (e.g., some African or Asian teams) should show more volatility. I also expected regional clusters: European nations densely occupying the top ranks, while Oceania and some African nations clustering lower. The visualization confirmed many of these expectations: long-term leaders displayed gentle slopes with short downward spikes after major tournaments, and several smaller nations showed sharp jumps after a few successful matches. One portion of the chart that matched expectations was the steady plateau of top European teams from 2010–2018, reflecting their consistent results in qualifiers and tournaments.

4. Surprises / Outliers

Some findings deviated from expectations. A few nations outside the traditional powerhouses showed abrupt, sustained increases in points that were not tied to widely publicized tournament successes. These outliers often corresponded to periods where a team played many friendlies against lower-ranked opponents (which can inflate points) or where FIFA ranking calculation changes temporarily favored certain match patterns. Another surprise was the magnitude of volatility for mid-ranked teams in certain confederations; instead of gradual change, several teams experienced wild oscillations, suggesting irregular match scheduling or occasional long gaps between competitive fixtures.

5. Questions for Further Investigation

  • Do sudden ranking increases correspond to clusters of friendlies versus competitive matches? (check match type)
  • Are there scheduling artifacts (long inactive periods) that amplify ranking volatility for some countries?
  • How do FIFA formula changes or point weighting adjustments explain sudden regional shifts?
  • Can pairing match-level results with ranking changes reveal causative matches or tournaments?

6. Tool Evaluation — Plotly

Plotly is excellent for creating interactive, web-ready visualizations directly in Python. It provides a high-level API for building charts like line plots, scatter plots, and more, with built-in interactivity such as zooming, panning, and tooltips. Plotly's strengths include ease of integration with Jupyter notebooks, support for complex data transformations, and the ability to export static or interactive HTML. However, limitations exist: it can be resource-intensive for very large datasets, and customizing advanced interactions requires JavaScript knowledge. Compared to Tableau, Plotly offers more programmatic control and reproducibility, but Tableau's drag-and-drop interface is faster for quick prototyping. Overall, Plotly is ideal for data scientists who want to combine analysis with presentation in code.

Self-Assessment

Dataset fit: I chose a dataset I know reasonably well which helped form expectations. Design reasoning: I selected temporal and categorical dimensions to investigate both longitudinal trends and regional patterns, and I can explain why each visual element exists. Analytical honesty: I documented expected patterns before deep exploration and then compared them with the visualization. Curiosity: The follow-up questions reflect genuine interest in understanding the mechanisms behind outliers. Tool evaluation specificity: My comments are based on actual interaction with Plotly during this exercise. Overall, this page shows work I would present in a portfolio: the interactive viz plus a clear written reflection framing the analysis and next steps.
