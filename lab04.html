Reflection & Analysis

1. Data Preparation

I selected a dataset I am familiar with: the FIFA world ranking dataset (international men's football rankings). Uploading datasets to Tableau Public can fail for a few common reasons: malformed CSV (unescaped quotes, inconsistent column counts), missing header row, or unexpected character encodings. To prepare the file I inspected the CSV in a text editor and loaded it into Python to verify consistent columns and UTF-8 encoding. I removed extraneous rows (notes or metadata at the top) and ensured dates were in ISO format (YYYY-MM-DD) so Tableau recognized them as dates. For geographic maps I verified country names matched Tableau's canonical country list; when necessary I created a standardized country code column using ISO3 codes to avoid ambiguous names.

2. Design Choices

For the visualization I focused on patterns in national team ranking changes over time. The primary metric is the FIFA ranking points and the derived metric of rank change (delta from previous ranking). Dimensions include Country (discrete), Date (continuous), and Region/Confederation as a grouping variable. I chose a line chart with multiple small multiples — one line per country — arranged by confederation, allowing comparison of temporal trends. For highlight interactions I configured tooltips to show rank, points, and recent match results (when available). Color encodes confederation to surface regional patterns, while line thickness highlights the top-tier teams to focus attention.

3. Expected Patterns

Based on my domain knowledge, I expected established football powers (e.g., Brazil, Germany, Argentina) to show relatively stable, high ranking points with occasional dips after poor tournament performance. Emerging teams (e.g., some African or Asian teams) should show more volatility. I also expected regional clusters: European nations densely occupying the top ranks, while Oceania and some African nations clustering lower. The visualization confirmed many of these expectations: long-term leaders displayed gentle slopes with short downward spikes after major tournaments, and several smaller nations showed sharp jumps after a few successful matches. One portion of the chart that matched expectations was the steady plateau of top European teams from 2010–2018, reflecting their consistent results in qualifiers and tournaments.

4. Surprises / Outliers

Some findings deviated from expectations. A few nations outside the traditional powerhouses showed abrupt, sustained increases in points that were not tied to widely publicized tournament successes. These outliers often corresponded to periods where a team played many friendlies against lower-ranked opponents (which can inflate points) or where FIFA ranking calculation changes temporarily favored certain match patterns. Another surprise was the magnitude of volatility for mid-ranked teams in certain confederations; instead of gradual change, several teams experienced wild oscillations, suggesting irregular match scheduling or occasional long gaps between competitive fixtures.

5. Questions for Further Investigation

  • Do sudden ranking increases correspond to clusters of friendlies versus competitive matches? (check match type)
  • Are there scheduling artifacts (long inactive periods) that amplify ranking volatility for some countries?
  • How do FIFA formula changes or point weighting adjustments explain sudden regional shifts?
  • Can pairing match-level results with ranking changes reveal causative matches or tournaments?

6. Tool Evaluation — Tableau Public

Tableau Public is strong at rapid exploration and interactive presentation. Drag-and-drop data binding, built-in geographic recognition, and easy interactive filters make it straightforward to prototype multi-view dashboards. Tableau's strengths include polished tooltips, responsive interactivity, and quick publishing to the web. However, limitations exist: advanced statistical transformations are less convenient than in Python/R (though calculated fields are powerful for many tasks), and fine-grained control over layout or web embedding behaviour sometimes requires manual HTML/CSS adjustments after publishing. Tableau Public also requires a published public copy (no private publishing), which matters for sensitive datasets. Compared to coding in D3 or building with Plotly via Python, Tableau accelerates iteration and storytelling, but programmatic reproducibility and version control are easier when using code-first workflows.

Reflection — Data Preparation, Design, and Findings

This project used the hometown_locations - Sheet1.csv dataset: a list of places from my hometown with names, addresses, category/type, short personal descriptions, and image URLs. The dataset is primarily categorical and textual; there are no native numeric measures, and the fields require a bit of cleanup before a smooth Tableau Public import.

Data preparation challenges: The CSV contained inconsistent quoting and delimiter placement which prevents reliable parsing by Tableau. Some rows had mis-placed quote characters, missing commas before the image URL, and inconsistent use of whitespace. Category labels had inconsistent capitalization (e.g., "Cultural" vs " Cultural"). Addresses include commas inside the field which is fine if quoting is correct, but in this file the quotes are inconsistent. Because Tableau expects a well-formed CSV or Excel file, I cleaned the file by: (1) normalizing quotes; (2) ensuring five columns with headers: Name, Address, Category, Description, ImageURL; (3) trimming whitespace and fixing obvious typos; (4) optionally adding Latitude and Longitude columns by geocoding addresses for reliable mapping in Tableau.

Design choices and reasoning: For this dataset I focused on two complementary visualization approaches: a symbol map and a category-count bar chart. The symbol map (using geocoded coordinates or Tableau's built-in geocoding if you extract City/State/Zip) best reveals spatial clustering, showing where parks, restaurants, and cultural sites concentrate. The bar chart of counts per category (Restaurant, Park, Historical, Cultural, School, Recreation) quickly surfaces dominant types and helps spot underrepresented categories. I also used short description length as a small numeric proxy (description word count) to highlight unusually long or short entries — useful to flag outliers or entries that might contain multiple locations mashed together.

Expected patterns: I expected restaurants and cultural locations to cluster around downtown Roswell (Canton St), parks to be more spread out near green spaces and the river, and historical sites to be near the Roswell Mill area. I also expected duplicate or near-duplicate addresses for sites that share the same location (e.g., Roswell Mill and Old Mill Park both listing 95 Mill St).

Surprises and outliers: Malformed rows and missing commas were the biggest surprise — these are not data insights but data-quality obstacles. In the cleaned visualization, outliers to look for include locations geocoded outside the expected city bounds (signaling an address parsing problem), categories with a single item, or descriptions that include extra URLs stuck to text (those indicate missing separators).

Questions for further investigation: Why do some categories cluster more than others? Are certain types of places (e.g., parks) consistently located near rivers or trails? Do description sentiments vary by category (could do a simple sentiment score on descriptions)?

Evaluation of Tableau Public: Tableau Public excels at quick spatial and categorical exploration with minimal code; it provides strong mapping and drag-and-drop chart building, which is ideal for this dataset. Limitations: sensitive to malformed CSVs, less flexible for advanced text-cleaning (preprocessing outside Tableau is recommended), and requires careful geocoding for precise mapping if addresses are full street addresses. Overall, Tableau Public is excellent for rapid prototyping and sharing interactive visuals for a portfolio.
