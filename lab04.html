Lab 4 — Interactive Visualization

Exploring patterns with Plotly

Interactive Charts (Plotly)

Select countries to compare their FIFA ranking points over time. This chart is generated locally using the cleaned CSV included in the repository.

Reflection & Analysis

1. Data Preparation

I chose a dataset I was already familiar with because I played soccer growing up and even traveled to London to play. Because of that, the FIFA men’s world rankings made sense to me and were easier to understand. I knew data files can cause problems if they are messy, missing labels, or formatted incorrectly. To prepare the dataset, I opened the CSV file in a text editor and also checked it in Python to make sure the columns lined up correctly. I removed extra rows at the top that were not part of the actual data and made sure the dates were all written the same way so they would load properly. For the interactive charts, I checked that numbers and dates were read correctly and fixed any missing values.

2. Design Choices

For the visualization, I focused on how national team rankings change over time. The main value I used was FIFA ranking points, along with how much a team’s ranking changed from one update to the next. The data includes the country, the date, and each team’s region or confederation. I used a line chart, with one line for each country, grouped by confederation, so it is easier to compare trends over time. I added tooltips so you can see details like ranking, points, and recent match results when you hover. Different colors show different confederations, and thicker lines highlight the top teams so they stand out more.

3. Expected Patterns

Based on what I already knew about international football, I expected teams like Brazil, Germany, and Argentina to stay near the top of the rankings most of the time, with small drops after bad tournament results. I also expected newer or less established teams, especially from parts of Africa and Asia, to move up and down more because their results are less consistent. I thought European teams would mostly fill the top spots, while teams from Oceania and some African countries would be lower overall. The chart mostly matched what I expected. Strong teams stayed fairly steady, with short drops after major tournaments, while some smaller teams jumped up quickly after a few good matches. One clear example was how top European teams stayed at about the same level from 2010 to 2018, showing how consistent their results were during that time.

4. Surprises / Outliers

Some of the results did not match what I expected. A few teams that are not usually considered top programs showed big jumps in points that did not line up with major tournament wins. In many cases, this seemed to happen during stretches where teams played a lot of matches against lower-ranked opponents, which can boost points. Another surprise was how much some mid-ranked teams moved up and down in the rankings. Instead of slow changes, their rankings swung a lot, which may be because they played matches unevenly or had long breaks between games.

5. Questions for Further Investigation

  • Do sudden ranking jumps happen when teams play a lot of friendly matches instead of competitive games?
  • Do long breaks between matches cause bigger ups and downs in rankings for some teams?
  • Did changes to the FIFA ranking formula help explain sudden shifts in certain regions?
  • Would matching individual game results with ranking changes show which games or tournaments caused the biggest changes?

6. Tool Evaluation — Plotly

Plotly is a good tool for making interactive charts using Python. It makes it easy to create things like line charts and scatter plots, and it automatically includes features like zooming, hovering, and tooltips. Plotly works well with Jupyter notebooks and lets you export charts as HTML, which makes them easy to put on a website. One downside is that it can be slow when working with very large datasets, and more advanced interactions can be hard to customize without knowing JavaScript. Compared to Tableau, Plotly gives more control through code, but Tableau is faster when you just want to quickly build a chart using drag-and-drop. Overall, Plotly is a good option for people who want to analyze data and present it in the same workflow.

Self-Assessment

I picked a dataset I already knew, which helped me have clear expectations going in. I chose to focus on time and categories so I could look at long-term trends and regional patterns, and I can explain why each part of the chart is there. Before really digging into the data, I wrote down what I expected to see and then compared that to what the visualization actually showed. The follow-up questions come from real curiosity about why some teams behaved differently than expected. My thoughts on Plotly are based on actually using it for this project. Overall, this page shows the kind of work I would include in a portfolio, with both an interactive chart and a clear written explanation of what I found and what I would explore next.
